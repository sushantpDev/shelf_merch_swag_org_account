# Corporate Gifting Budget: A Practical Plan for Multiple Teams

A corporate gifting budget should explain how much your organization intends to commit, which teams can use it, and how that budget reaches recipients. For a program that includes company stores and employee points, the plan should also distinguish an allocated budget from points distributed and merchandise ordered.

ShelfMerch's enterprise workflow starts with a company admin and an assigned company budget. The company creates entity managers, allocates budgets to them, and uses those allocations for stores, kits, and employee points. Employees can then redeem points for merchandise through a store, with ShelfMerch receiving and processing the resulting orders.

Here is a practical way to plan that program across multiple teams without assuming that every team has the same needs.

## Define what the budget is meant to support

Begin with a program brief. List the employee groups involved, the intended gifting occasions, and whether your team plans to offer employee choice, send curated kits, or discuss both approaches.

Separate confirmed needs from ideas that still require a decision. An ongoing merch program and a single welcome-kit initiative may share a budget conversation, but they can involve different recipients, product selections, and operating responsibilities.

Your initial brief should include:

- The purpose of each program.
- The intended recipient group.
- The person accountable for planning it.
- The proposed store or kit approach.
- Questions about products, service coverage, and commercial terms.

This is a planning document. It does not need to assume a point conversion rate, a standard gift value, or a specific product price before those details are confirmed.

## Map the company budget to entity responsibilities

The company-level budget and entity allocations serve different purposes. The first provides an overall frame for the program. The second assigns a portion of that frame to a manager's area of responsibility.

Before assigning amounts, agree how your company wants to organize the program. Confirm which groups belong together and who will act as each entity manager. Make those responsibilities clear enough that employees and internal stakeholders know where to take questions.

### Use a labeled planning example

As an illustration, a company might plan separate allocations for a new hire program, an employee merch initiative, and a team gifting campaign. Each allocation would have a named owner and a defined purpose.

This example is not a ShelfMerch configuration rule or a recommended split. Your allocation structure should reflect your organization and the setup agreed during onboarding. There is no need to invent a currency-to-points formula to discuss who owns each part of the budget.

### Record the reason behind an allocation

For each area, write down the audience, purpose, and assumptions behind the planned amount. If those assumptions change, the program owner can revisit the decision with the relevant stakeholders.

For example, a change in the intended recipient group may require a fresh product and budget conversation. The point of recording assumptions is to support that conversation rather than letting an old allocation become an unexplained default.

## Keep budget allocations, employee points, and orders separate

In the ShelfMerch workflow, managers distribute employee points from allocated budgets. Employees then use points to redeem merchandise through a store. The resulting orders move to ShelfMerch for processing.

These are connected stages, but they are not interchangeable measures.

### Budget allocated

An allocation identifies the budget assigned to a manager's area. It describes responsibility for a part of the program. It does not mean that employees have already received all their points or ordered merchandise.

### Points distributed

Points distribution gives employees the means to participate in the agreed redemption program. Your team should confirm the applicable point rules and explain them clearly to employees.

Do not assume a standard point value, expiry period, transfer policy, or payment combination. Ask how those questions apply to the program you are setting up.

### Merchandise ordered

An employee order records a later action: the employee has chosen merchandise and completed the store order workflow. ShelfMerch receives and processes those orders.

When reviewing the program, distinguish the stages in your own working notes and ask which records or reporting views are available. This guide does not assume a particular dashboard, export, or accounting treatment.

## Plan stores and kits around the intended experience

A store starts with a selection of catalog products and gives employees a place to choose merchandise. A kit starts with a curated product selection. Both require a budget conversation, but the questions you ask may differ.

For a store, consider the selection available to the employee and how the planned points allocation relates to that experience. For a kit, consider the intended recipients, the products you want included, and the information needed to prepare the program.

Confirm current catalog options and the applicable commercial details with ShelfMerch. Avoid setting expectations from a past campaign, a competitor's pricing, or a generic estimate that does not match the actual products.

## Discuss costs and service terms before announcing the program

Before communicating a gifting program to employees, ask for the details relevant to your actual order requirements. Depending on the proposal, your team may need clarity on product costs, branding, packaging, delivery, and other applicable charges.

Treat these as questions for confirmation. The presence of a budget or points feature does not establish what a quote includes or how a charge will be handled.

If your finance team has specific reporting or approval requirements, bring them to the demo. Ask to see how the proposed workflow relates to those requirements and identify anything your team will handle separately. Do not assume that an internal policy is automatically enforced by the platform.

## Review the plan with the people who will run it

A useful budget plan connects the people responsible for the company program, the entity managers, and the employee experience. Before launch, review the same brief together so that everyone is working from the same assumptions.

Check that each manager understands the intended audience and allocation. Review the products or kit approach, the employee communication, and the order-processing handoff. Resolve unclear policies before they become questions during the program.

After the program begins, use the information available to your team to review participation and order activity. Discuss changes based on actual program needs. Avoid treating an assigned allocation as proof that the intended employee experience has already happened.
